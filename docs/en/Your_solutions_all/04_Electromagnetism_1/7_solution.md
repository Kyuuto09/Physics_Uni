# Problem 7: Cyclotron Motion

### 1. Problem Statement

An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 5000 V. It then enters a region of uniform magnetic field B = 0.1 T, perpendicular to its velocity. What is the radius of the circular path it will follow?

---

### 2. Solution and Explanation

**Concept Intuition:**
This is a classic two-part problem describing exactly how a particle accelerator or an old CRT television perfectly works! 
First, the electron rests at the starting line. A giant 5000-Volt electric field forces it to rigidly accelerate, flawlessly converting invisible electrical potential energy into absolute physical kinetic energy (speed). 
Once it totally escapes the electric field and enters the magnetic field, something fundamentally distinct happens. The *Lorentz Force* generated by a magnetic field always pushes *perpendicularly* to the direction the particle is moving. This continuous, exactly-sideways pushing acts identically to a rigid string tied to a spinning ball; it effortlessly forces the electron into a precise circular orbit (centripetal force) without ever actually speeding it up or mathematically slowing it down!

#### Part A: Electric Acceleration

When an electron (with fundamental charge $e$) passes perfectly through a voltage ($V$), it converts its electrical Potential Energy ($U_e = eV$) entirely into Kinetic Energy ($K = \frac{1}{2}m_ev^2$):
$$eV = \frac{1}{2}m_ev^2$$

We can beautifully solve this algebraically for its final velocity ($v$):
$$v^2 = \frac{2eV}{m_e}$$
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m_e}}$$

*(Pedagogical Note: Because $5000\text{ V}$ is significantly less than the electron's rest mass scale of $\approx 511,000\text{ eV}$, we can safely ignore Einstein's theory of relativistic mass increase for this particular problem.)*

#### Part B: The Magnetic Circle

Once precisely inside the magnetic field ($B$), the electron experiences the heavy magnetic Lorentz force:
$$F_B = evB$$
*(Since the given velocity and magnetic field are perfectly perpendicular, $\sin(90^\circ) = 1$.)*

According to Newton's universally applied Second Law, this continuously sideways force perfectly functions as the centripetal force holding it in a tight circular path of radius $R$:
$$F_B = F_c$$
$$evB = \frac{m_ev^2}{R}$$

We can instantly cleanly cancel exactly one $v$ from symmetrically both sides, then logically solve for the radius $R$:
$$eB = \frac{m_ev}{R}$$
$$R = \frac{m_ev}{eB}$$

#### Part C: Algebraic Combination

Rather than needlessly calculating large fragile intermediate speeds, we can simply and exactly substitute our mathematically perfect $v$ equation from Part A directly into our radius equation!
$$R = \frac{m_e}{eB} \sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m_e}}$$

We can elegantly push the outside mass/charge variables entirely inside the square root by rigorously squaring them:
$$R = \frac{1}{B} \sqrt{\frac{2eV \cdot m_e^2}{m_e \cdot e^2}}$$
$$R = \frac{1}{B} \sqrt{\frac{2Vm_e}{e}}$$

#### Part D: Numerical Calculation

Now we precisely plug in our standard constant values reliably:
- $V = 5000 \text{ V}$
- $B = 0.1 \text{ T}$
- $m_e \approx 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}$
- $e \approx 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$

$$R = \frac{1}{0.1} \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 5000 \cdot (9.11 \times 10^{-31})}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}}}$$
$$R = 10 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{9.11 \times 10^{-27}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}}}$$
$$R = 10 \cdot \sqrt{5.69 \times 10^{-8}}$$
$$R = 10 \cdot (2.39 \times 10^{-4})$$
$$R \approx 2.39 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}$$

Converting standardized meters to millimeters is extremely insightful, as this flawlessly visualizes a real-world realistic loop size.
$R = 2.39 \text{ mm}$

---

### 3. Final Answers

- **Velocity leaving acceleration:** $\approx 4.19 \times 10^7 \text{ m/s}$ (implied sub-step)
- **Radius of perfectly circular path ($R$):** $\approx 2.39 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}$ or more intuitively **$2.39 \text{ mm}$**.
